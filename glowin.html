01Context

When expansion becomes a consistency problem

When a platform serves multiple entry points (POS, online booking and AI phone intake), the real design challenge isn't adding features. It's making sure everything behaves like one system.


As the product expanded, the challenge shifted from building new entry points to keeping behavior consistent across all of them.


Two counts run through this case study. 3 channels are how a booking comes in: POS / walk-in, online, and AI phone. 4 surfaces are the screens people act on: the customer booking web, iPad POS, Sunmi handheld, and Boss App. The AI phone is a channel without a screen of its own; its output lands on the merchant surfaces.


Glowin serves both beauty and food & beverage merchants on the same platform. This case study follows a salon's booking flow; the Handheld and Boss App previews show a restaurant merchant, because those surfaces are shared across both verticals.

02Problem Shift

Three core challenges emerged

Where it started. The existing iPad app showed every order as the same card: a number, a name, a time, an amount and "Unpaid". It didn't show where a booking came from or what stage it was at, so staff couldn't tell an online request from a walk-in without opening it.

01
Intake logic
Different sources had different timing, validation needs, and confidence levels, so the same request could behave differently depending on where it came from.
02
State consistency
Once inside operations, requests couldn't be treated as separate flows. Inconsistent handling meant staff had to compensate manually.
03
Cross-surface visibility
Merchants needed a clear view of source, status, and next actions. Without it, staying on top of activity meant constant context-switching.
03How the Work Moved

Flows first, then screens, then rules

Selected entries from my work log, February to August 2025. The early rounds were mostly visual. From spring on, most of the work was about how the system should behave, and nearly every round started from a review with the team.

  1. Feb 2025
    Website and first app screens

    The marketing site for mobile and desktop, then the main app screens. After review, the second version pushed navigation back so order categories lead, moved every page to a horizontal navigation bar, and reworked New Order to fit more products on one screen.

  2. Mar 2025
    Flows before screens

    I mapped the online booking web flow and the POS booking workflow and collected references from existing booking systems before drawing anything. The mobile booking screens were then revised after a team meeting, and the web version followed.

  3. Mar – Apr 2025
    Booking on the iPad

    Notifications, booking details and the merchant workflow on iPad, plus staff schedules and business hours, revised over two rounds of product feedback.

  4. Apr – May 2025
    Rules become settings

    Booking rules became settings each merchant controls (Decision 04). A broken booking link now leads to contact details, the shop's phone number and the home page instead of a dead end. Checkout gained tips and a signature step, then was revised after a product review.

  5. May 2025
    The AI phone line

    I mapped the AI call workflow: answer by voice, check the scheduling engine against the POS booking table, create the booking and send an SMS confirmation, handle rescheduling, and pick up the caller's basic details.

  6. Jun 2025
    Payment rules

    Tax and split payment moved into checkout as rules set once per shop (Decision 05).

  7. Jun – Aug 2025
    Boss App

    Structure and wireframes for the owner's remote app, a few options compared, then finalized.

04Architectural Strategy

Channel-specific intake.
Consistent downstream behavior.

Three distinct entry points, each with its own rules at intake, converge into one operational model. The system stays flexible where channels differ, predictable where merchants need to act.

AI Phone
AI Phone Booking
Generated through AI‑assisted phone calls. Auto‑accepted, AI‑tagged for tracking.
Online
Online Booking
Submitted by customers. Requires staff review before confirmation enters the queue.
POS
Merchant-Created Booking
Created directly by staff. Immediate confidence: auto‑confirmed, enters the queue instantly.

Two mechanisms kept the system coherent: source labeling (each request tagged by channel, visible at a glance) and state management (consistent status behavior across all entry points).

07Design Decisions

The real work was in
rules, states, and edge cases

Decision 01 · Source labels
Keep channel identity, and show it everywhere
Rather than normalizing all channels at intake, I kept each booking's channel as a label that travels with it. Different intake rules could apply without fragmenting operations, and staff could read the source at a glance instead of investigating. That was a prerequisite for cutting the manual compensation that was already happening.
Decision 02 · One state model
One explicit set of statuses for every channel
Whatever the channel, a booking moves through the same statuses: Pending, Confirmed, In Progress, Completed. Merchants could trust the behavior without needing to understand the logic behind each channel.
Decision 03 · Rules as shared reference
The rule table as the team's reference point
Channel rules lived in one table, the one in section 04. New features were checked against it before they were designed, which changed how the PM, engineering and I made decisions together.
Decision 04 · Rules as settings
Booking rules the merchant owns
Every shop runs its day differently, so buffer time between appointments, the slot interval and blocked hours became settings rather than constants. The booking web reads them, so customers only see times the shop can actually take.
Decision 05 · Payment rules
Tax and split payment, defined once
Tax is a percentage with one master switch and a setting for each payment method: cash, credit card and gift card. Split payment sits directly under the total, at the point where the bill is settled.

Out of scope here. Staff scheduling and assignment, meaning availability and who takes which booking, run on the calendar's time grid. They are not part of the state model this case study covers.

08Intended Outcomes

Designed so the logic would be self-evident

This was a 0→1 startup build. We ran no formal usability testing and tracked no metrics for these changes, so I don't report measured results here. Each card pairs a design decision with the outcome it was built for, what we actually noticed, and how I would measure it next time.

Decision 01 · Source labels
Where a booking came from is visible on every surface
Built forStaff can read the source and next action without opening the booking.
What we noticedIn sales demos and early onboarding, this part needed less explaining. Informal, not measured.
How I'd measure itTime to first action on a new booking; wrong confirmations in a task-based test.
Decision 02 · One state model
Every channel lands in the same set of statuses
Built forRemoving the manual workarounds staff used when channels behaved differently.
What we noticedFeedback from a small group of early customers fed straight into the rule table.
How I'd measure itManual status corrections per week, before vs. after the state model.
Decision 03 · Rules as shared reference
New features are checked against the rule table first
Built forA shared way for PM, engineering and design to decide how a new feature should behave.
What we noticedReviews started asking "does this fit the system?" more often than "what should this do?"
How I'd measure itChannel-specific exceptions added per release.
09Reflection

I initially approached this as an interface problem, but the real work turned out to be in rules, states, and edge cases. Working in a startup where both the PM and I were doing this for the first time, we relied heavily on early customers: a small group, but loyal ones whose feedback shaped a lot of the decisions. We figured things out as we went, which meant the system had to be flexible enough to absorb changes without breaking existing behavior.
